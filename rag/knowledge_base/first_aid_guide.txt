First-Aid and Emergency Symptom Recognition Guide

Call emergency services immediately (for example 911 in the US and Canada, 112 in the EU and India, 999 in the UK, or 000 in Australia) if you or someone else experiences any of the following:

Possible heart attack: Chest pain, pressure, tightness or squeezing that may spread to the arm, shoulder, jaw, neck or back, sometimes with cold sweat, nausea or shortness of breath. Sit or lie the person down, keep them calm and still, loosen tight clothing, and do not let them drive themselves. If an emergency operator advises chewing aspirin, only do so if the person is not allergic.

Possible stroke - remember FAST: Face drooping on one side, Arm weakness or numbness (especially on one side), Speech slurred or difficult to understand, Time to call emergency services immediately, because treatment is time-critical. Also watch for sudden confusion, sudden trouble seeing, or sudden severe dizziness or loss of balance. Note the time symptoms began and give no food, drink or medicine.

Severe difficulty breathing: Sudden or severe shortness of breath, wheezing, an inability to speak in full sentences, or lips and fingertips turning blue. Sit the person upright, loosen tight clothing, and use a prescribed reliever inhaler or adrenaline auto-injector if one is available and prescribed.

Severe or uncontrolled bleeding: Bleeding that soaks through clothing or bandages and does not stop with firm direct pressure. Apply firm pressure with a clean cloth, add more layers rather than removing a soaked one, and keep the injured area raised if possible.

Severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis): Swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat, widespread hives, sudden difficulty breathing, or a sudden drop in blood pressure (dizziness, fainting), often after a new food, sting or medicine. Use an adrenaline auto-injector immediately if one has been prescribed, and lay the person flat unless breathing is difficult.

Suspected overdose or poisoning: Taking far more than the maximum label dose of any medicine (including paracetamol, which can cause serious, sometimes silent liver damage) or swallowing a chemical or unknown substance. Do not make the person vomit. Call emergency services or a poison control centre right away and keep the packaging to show responders.

Unresponsiveness, seizures or fainting: If someone is unconscious but breathing normally, place them on their side in the recovery position. If a seizure occurs, cushion the head, do not restrain the person or put anything in their mouth, and time the seizure; call for help if it lasts more than five minutes or repeats.
