WHO Fact Sheet: Pain Management
Paracetamol (acetaminophen) is an analgesic and antipyretic used for mild-to-moderate pain and fever. It is generally gentle on the stomach but its main danger is liver damage from exceeding the maximum daily dose, including from multiple products that each contain paracetamol.
Ibuprofen, naproxen and aspirin are nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) used for pain, fever and inflammation. NSAIDs can irritate the stomach lining, raise blood pressure, cause fluid retention and affect the kidneys, especially with regular or high-dose use, and should be taken with food.

WHO Fact Sheet: Cardiovascular Health and Hypertension
Hypertension (high blood pressure) is a leading risk factor for heart attack, stroke and kidney damage, and it often causes no symptoms, so regular measurement is the only reliable way to detect it. Common classes of blood pressure medication include ACE inhibitors (such as lisinopril), angiotensin receptor blockers or ARBs (such as losartan), diuretics (such as hydrochlorothiazide), calcium channel blockers (such as amlodipine) and beta-blockers (such as atenolol).
NSAIDs such as ibuprofen, naproxen and aspirin can raise blood pressure, cause fluid retention and reduce the effectiveness of blood pressure medications, particularly with regular use; occasional low-dose use is a smaller concern than daily use. People on blood pressure medication who need regular pain relief should ask a doctor or pharmacist which pain reliever is most suitable.
Statins such as atorvastatin lower LDL cholesterol and reduce the risk of heart attack and stroke; large amounts of grapefruit juice can raise blood levels of some statins and increase the risk of muscle-related side effects.

WHO Fact Sheet: Antimicrobial Resistance and Antibiotic Use
Antibiotics such as amoxicillin work only against bacterial infections, not against colds, flu or most sore throats, which are usually caused by viruses. Overuse and misuse of antibiotics is a leading driver of antimicrobial resistance, which makes future infections harder and more expensive to treat. Patients should take antibiotics only when prescribed, complete the full course as directed, and never share or save leftover antibiotics.

WHO Fact Sheet: Diabetes
Type 2 diabetes is a long-term condition in which the body cannot use insulin effectively, causing blood sugar to rise; it is commonly managed with diet, physical activity and medicines such as metformin. Kidney function should be monitored during metformin treatment, and it may need to be paused temporarily around certain contrast-dye scans, severe dehydration or heavy alcohol use. Warning signs of low blood sugar include shakiness, sweating, confusion and hunger; warning signs of very high blood sugar include extreme thirst, frequent urination and drowsiness, and either should prompt following your care plan or seeking help.

WHO Fact Sheet: Generic Medicines
A generic medicine contains the same active ingredient as the branded original, in the same strength, form and route of administration, and must meet the same regulatory standards for quality and effect. Generic medicines are typically less expensive than the branded original because manufacturers do not repeat the original developer's clinical trials and compete on price. Inactive ingredients such as coatings or fillers can differ between brands, which rarely matters except for people with specific allergies; a pharmacist can confirm the active ingredient and strength before a brand switch.
